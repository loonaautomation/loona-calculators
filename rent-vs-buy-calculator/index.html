⚖️ Rent vs Buy Calculator

“Rent is money thrown away” and “a house is always an investment” are both too simple. The honest answer depends on how long you will stay, what rent does over time, and how much equity you build. Enter your own numbers and this calculator compares the true cost of both choices over your stay.

How to use the Rent vs Buy Calculator

  1. Enter the price of the home and the down payment you would make.
  2. Add the mortgage rate and term, and the rent you would otherwise pay for a similar home.
  3. Set how many years you expect to stay, how fast rent rises each year, and how fast home prices grow.
  4. Press Compare — the verdict shows which option costs less over your stay, and by how much.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it better to rent or buy a house?

It depends mainly on how long you will stay and how prices and rents move. Buying has heavy upfront costs and the early mortgage payments are mostly interest, so short stays usually favour renting. The longer you stay, the more equity you build and the more buying pulls ahead. Run your own numbers above — the stay length alone can flip the answer.

What is the 5-year rule for buying a home?

A rough guideline that buying usually only starts to beat renting after about five years in the same home, because the upfront costs and the interest-heavy early payments need time to be spread out and offset by equity and price growth. It is only a guideline — high rent growth or fast price growth can shorten it, and flat prices can stretch it well beyond five years.

Does this calculator include property tax and buying costs?

Ongoing costs are covered through the maintenance & insurance percentage, which you can raise to include property tax. One-off costs — stamp duty, registration, legal fees and selling commission — are not in the default model. They are real, and they punish short stays; if they matter in your case, treat the buying result as slightly optimistic.

How it works — the formula

Renting is a geometric series: each year the rent rises by your increase percentage, so the total is the sum of every year's rent over your stay. Buying is compared on a net basis — everything you pay out, minus what you get back. The mortgage payment uses the standard annuity formula (monthly rate = annual rate ÷ 1200). At the end of your stay the home is sold at its appreciated value; what you keep is that value minus the loan balance still owed (your equity).

Formulas: Rent total = Σ monthly rent × (1 + increase)year × 12. Monthly payment = Loan × r × (1 + r)n ÷ ((1 + r)n − 1). Buy net cost = down payment + mortgage payments during the stay + maintenance & insurance − (value at sale − remaining balance).

Worked example

Inputs: Home price Rs 80,00,000; down payment 25% (Rs 20,00,000); mortgage 9% over 15 years; rent Rs 30,000/month rising 5% a year; stay of 5 years; price growth 4% a year; maintenance & insurance 1% of price a year.

Steps: The loan is Rs 60,00,000 and the monthly payment is Rs 60,856. Renting costs Rs 19,89,227 over the 5 years. Buying: Rs 20,00,000 down + Rs 36,51,360 of payments + Rs 4,00,000 maintenance = Rs 60,51,360 paid out; the home grows to Rs 97,33,223, the loan balance is Rs 48,04,075, so the equity returned is Rs 49,29,148 — a net cost of Rs 11,22,212.

Result: Over 5 years, buying is cheaper by Rs 8,67,016 in this example. Change the stay to 2 years or the growth to 0% and the answer flips — which is exactly why the inputs are yours to set.

Frequently asked questions

Why does the length of my stay change the answer so much?

When you rent, every payment is gone for good but there is nothing to set up. When you buy, the down payment and fees go out on day one, while the benefit — equity — builds slowly at first because early payments are mostly interest. Time is what lets appreciation on the full home value and the falling loan balance work in your favour.

What price growth rate should I enter?

Use a conservative long-run figure for your city — for many areas 3–5% a year is a reasonable planning range, roughly in line with long-term inflation. Avoid using the hottest recent year as your assumption; if the answer only works at 8–10% growth, it is the growth doing the work, not the purchase.
