🧮 P-Value Calculator

Enter a test statistic — z, t (with degrees of freedom), or chi-square (with df) — and get the exact one-tailed and two-tailed p-values plus a plain verdict at the usual 0.05 and 0.01 levels.

Quick answer: A p-value is the probability of a result at least this extreme if the null hypothesis were true. z = 1.96 gives a two-tailed p of exactly about 0.0500; chi-square 3.841 with 1 df also gives p ≈ 0.0500.

How to use the P-Value Calculator

  1. Choose the statistic type: z, t, or chi-square.
  2. Enter the statistic's value (and degrees of freedom for t / chi-square).
  3. Read the right-tail, left-tail and two-tailed p-values.
Example: A study reports z = 2.58. Two-tailed p ≈ 0.0099 — significant at the 0.01 level. The same number as a one-tailed (right) test gives p ≈ 0.0049.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does a p-value NOT mean?

It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, and not the probability that your result is a fluke in plain terms. It measures how surprising the data would be if the null were true — nothing more, which is why effect sizes and confidence intervals matter alongside it.

One-tailed or two-tailed?

Two-tailed is the honest default: it counts extremes in both directions and must be chosen before seeing data. One-tailed tests are only defensible when the direction was fixed in advance and the other direction is genuinely impossible or irrelevant.

Is p = 0.049 really different from p = 0.051?

Barely. The 0.05 line is a convention, not a cliff in evidence. Good reporting gives the exact p-value, which is what this calculator returns.

What about chi-square direction?

Chi-square tests are inherently right-tailed: large statistics mean bigger departures from expected counts, so only the right-tail p-value applies.

How it works — the formula

P-values are read from each distribution's cumulative function: for z, Φ from the standard normal; for t, the t CDF via the incomplete beta function; for chi-square, the chi-square CDF via the incomplete gamma function. Two-tailed p for z/t = 2 × (1 − CDF(|statistic|)); chi-square uses the right tail only: p = 1 − CDF(statistic).

Worked example

Inputs: z = 1.96.

Steps: Φ(1.96) = 0.97500. Right tail = 1 − 0.97500 = 0.02500. Two-tailed = 2 × 0.02500 = 0.0500.

Result: Two-tailed p = 0.0500 — exactly on the conventional 0.05 line, which is why 1.96 is the famous critical value.
