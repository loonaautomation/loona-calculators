📐 Z-Test Calculator

Run a one-sample z-test for the case where the population standard deviation σ is genuinely known: enter the sample mean, σ, sample size and the hypothesised mean to get z and its p-values.

Quick answer: z = (x̄ − μ₀) ÷ (σ ÷ √n). Sample mean 105, σ = 15, n = 25, μ₀ = 100: z = 5 ÷ 3 = 1.667, two-tailed p ≈ 0.0956 — closer to significance than the t-test on the same numbers, because z assumes σ is known exactly.

How to use the Z-Test Calculator

  1. Enter the sample mean.
  2. Enter the known population standard deviation σ.
  3. Enter n and the hypothesised mean μ₀.
  4. Read z and the one- and two-tailed p-values.
Example: A machine fills bottles to a known process σ of 15 ml. A sample of 25 averages 105 against a 100 target: z = 1.667, p ≈ 0.0956 — watch the process, but the evidence does not cross the 5% line.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is σ ever 'known'?

In standardised testing with long-established scales, in manufacturing processes characterised over years of data, and in textbook problems. If σ was estimated from the same sample, the t-test is the correct tool.

Why is the z p-value smaller than the t p-value here?

Because knowing σ removes a layer of uncertainty. The t distribution's heavier tails at 24 df price in the chance that s underestimates σ; z does not pay that price.

What significance level should I use?

0.05 is conventional, 0.01 stricter. Choose before looking at results, and report the exact p-value alongside — this calculator always shows it.

How it works — the formula

z = (x̄ − μ₀) ÷ (σ ÷ √n). P-values come from the standard normal CDF Φ: two-tailed p = 2 × (1 − Φ(|z|)).

Worked example

Inputs: x̄ = 105, σ = 15, n = 25, μ₀ = 100.

Steps: Standard error = 15 ÷ 5 = 3. z = 5 ÷ 3 = 1.667. Φ(1.667) ≈ 0.9522. Two-tailed p = 2 × (1 − 0.9522) = 0.0956.

Result: z = 1.667, p ≈ 0.0956.
